Chapter 04

Health, Safety & the Body

Female soccer players tear ACLs at multiples of the male rate. The good news is that we know why — and the protocols that reduce the risk are free, well-documented, and almost nobody runs them properly.

If we could choose only one chapter for every coach, parent, and athletic director to read, this is the one. The medicine of girls' soccer has come a long way in twenty years. The application of that medicine — at the field, at the practice, at the dinner table — has not kept pace. The injuries we worry about most in this sport are largely predictable and substantially preventable, and the protocols are open-source.

This chapter is informational. Specific medical concerns belong with a physician, athletic trainer, or sports-medicine professional.

1. The ACL problem

Female soccer players sustain anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries at materially higher rates than male players in the same sport. Peer-reviewed estimates of the female-to-male ratio in soccer typically fall in the 2–3× range; some older studies and some sports (notably basketball) report higher multiples. Whatever the precise number, the direction is consistent and the implication is the same. The bulk of the risk is concentrated in the U13–U18 window, with peaks during and shortly after the adolescent growth spurt. The injury is overwhelmingly non-contact: a cut, a landing, a deceleration. The athlete plants, pivots, and the knee gives.

The mechanisms are well understood. They are biomechanical, hormonal, and developmental:

  • Q-angle and pelvis geometry. A wider pelvis (post-puberty) increases knee valgus — the knee collapsing inward — under load.
  • Quadriceps-dominant landing. Under-recruited hamstrings and glutes shift load forward across the knee.
  • Neuromuscular timing. A small lag in protective hamstring activation under deceleration disproportionately exposes the ACL.
  • The growth window. Peak height velocity is associated with temporary loss of motor control. Coaching and conditioning programs that ignore this window pay a tax.
  • Hormonal cycling. Some studies suggest ACL injury risk fluctuates with the menstrual cycle. Evidence is real but not yet decisive on operational implications. Awareness helps; tracking is increasingly common in elite environments.

2. What actually prevents ACLs

Multiple structured neuromuscular training programs have been shown, in randomized trials, to reduce ACL injury risk by 50–70% in female adolescent athletes. They share a common architecture: 15–25 minute warm-ups, three times per week, focused on jumping mechanics, deceleration, hamstring activation, and core stability.

ProgramOriginFormat
FIFA 11+ FIFA Medical Assessment & Research Centre 20-minute on-field warm-up. Three parts: running, strength & balance, plyometrics. Soccer-specific. Free.
PEP (Prevent Injury and Enhance Performance) Santa Monica Sports Medicine Foundation 15–20 minute program; the original randomized-trial protocol. Strong evidence base in female collegiate and youth populations.
KIPP Various Knee Injury Prevention Program — an alternative neuromuscular protocol used in some U.S. high school and club settings.

The protocols themselves are not the issue. Adoption is. Studies of program effectiveness consistently find that the benefit is contingent on the program being run as designed, three times a week, for the full season — not run as designed in week one and abandoned by week six. The single most useful question a parent can ask a club: "What injury-prevention protocol does my daughter's team run, and how often?"

What to look for in a warm-up

If the team's warm-up is mostly jogging, dynamic stretches, and ball work, the coach is treating injury prevention as an afterthought. A real protocol includes deceleration drills, single-leg landings with conscious knee tracking, hamstring-dominant exercises (Nordic curls, RDLs, or progressions), and plyometric work with emphasis on landing mechanics.

3. Concussion: heading, collisions, protocol

Girls' soccer has consistently ranked at or near the top of high-school sports for concussion rate, ahead of boys' soccer and competitive with sports like football and ice hockey when measured by athlete-exposures.

Heading

U.S. Soccer's heading guidelines, in place since 2015 and updated since, recommend:

  • No purposeful heading in practice or games for players age 10 and under.
  • Limited heading in training for ages 11–13.
  • Heading allowed at age 14 and above, with appropriate technique training.

The bigger drivers of concussion in girls' soccer, however, are not headers. They are head-to-head, head-to-elbow, head-to-ground, and (especially) goalkeeper collisions in the box. Heading reform is necessary but not sufficient.

The protocol

Modern concussion protocol, in plain English:

  1. If a head injury is suspected, the player comes off and stays off. No "shake it off." There is no in-game test that reliably rules out a concussion.
  2. Same-day evaluation by a qualified provider. Many states require it for high-school athletes.
  3. Symptom-guided rest for the first 24–48 hours, then a graded return to activity. The strict "dark room for a week" approach has been replaced by gradual, monitored return.
  4. Return to school before return to play. Full classroom function should be restored before contact training resumes.
  5. Stepwise return to play over multiple sessions: light aerobic, sport-specific, non-contact training, contact training, full play. Each step requires symptom-free function.
  6. Written clearance from a qualified provider before full return.

Baseline neurocognitive testing (ImPACT and similar) is now common in club and high-school settings. It's most useful as a comparison reference if a concussion later occurs.

4. Growth-related injury and overuse

The U10–U14 window is when the body grows fastest and the bones grow before the soft tissue catches up. The result is a category of injury particular to youth athletes:

  • Sever's disease (calcaneal apophysitis): Heel pain, common 8–13. Almost always self-limiting with rest, calf stretching, and heel cushions.
  • Osgood-Schlatter: Pain at the tibial tuberosity (just below the kneecap). Often around 10–14. Self-limiting; managed with load reduction and quad/hamstring work.
  • Sinding-Larsen-Johansson: Patellar tendon pain at the kneecap. Similar age, similar management.
  • Stress fractures. Increasing in incidence in young female athletes, often associated with energy-availability problems (see RED-S).
  • Hip impingement and groin pain. Sometimes related to repetitive kicking and rapid skeletal change.

The single-sport question

Sports medicine consensus on early specialization, especially for girls and especially for soccer, is unusually clear: playing one sport year-round before age 12 increases the risk of overuse injury and burnout, without measurable performance benefit. Multi-sport athletes through middle school perform at least as well as specialists by high school and are injured less.

"Year-round soccer" before puberty is not a developmental advantage. It is a marketing claim by clubs that need full-year revenue.

5. RED-S and the energy equation

RED-S — Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport — is the modern medical framework for understanding what happens when an athlete chronically takes in less energy than she expends. It expanded the older "Female Athlete Triad" (low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction, low bone density) into a more complete picture of metabolic and hormonal consequences.

Signs that warrant medical conversation, especially during the U13–U18 window:

  • Loss of menstrual cycles, irregular cycles, or delayed onset of menses.
  • Repeated stress fractures or unusually slow healing.
  • Persistent fatigue, mood changes, decline in training performance.
  • Frequent illness or slow recovery from minor injury.
  • Body-composition fixation, restrictive eating patterns, or rapid unintentional weight loss.

RED-S is not a diagnosis a parent or coach makes; it is a constellation of signs that warrants a sports-medicine and, often, a registered-dietitian conversation. Early intervention is the difference between a season and a career.

The performance lie

The myth that lighter is faster has done enormous damage to female athletes. Performance in soccer is built on strength, power, and durability. Underfueled athletes do not get faster; they get hurt. Coaches who comment on player bodies — even casually, even positively — should expect to be corrected. Important

6. Mental health and the athlete

Anxiety, depression, and disordered eating occur at meaningful rates in collegiate and elite youth populations, and the data on female athletes specifically warrants attention. Several practical points:

  • The transition out of sport is a known risk window. Whether due to injury, graduation, or simply not making the next team, transition is associated with elevated mental-health risk. Plan for it.
  • The "tough" culture is changing. A generation of professional players has spoken openly about therapy, anxiety, and burnout. The culture in elite environments is more honest now than it has ever been.
  • Sleep is a mental-health lever. Adolescents need 8–10 hours; high-performing youth athletes are often closer to 6–7 between training, school, and screens. The single highest-leverage intervention in many family budgets is moving practice end times earlier.
  • The car ride home matters. Most parents underestimate this. A short, low-pressure conversation after games — "I love watching you play" is the canonical sentence — is a higher-leverage parenting practice than tactical commentary.

If a player is in crisis, contact a qualified mental-health professional. In the U.S., 988 reaches the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text). The National Alliance for Eating Disorders runs a helpline; reach them at 1-866-662-1235 or via allianceforeatingdisorders.com.

7. Training load and recovery

Modern thinking on training load uses a simple rule of thumb: an adolescent athlete should not have more weekly hours of organized sport than her age in years. A 12-year-old should be at less than 12 hours/week of structured training and games combined. Above that, injury rates rise meaningfully.

The corollary, less often discussed: at least one full day per week should be unstructured. Pickup, bike, swim, hammock, doesn't matter. Recovery is when adaptation happens.

8. Return to play

The most preventable second injury is the one that happens when the player returns too soon. After any significant injury — ACL reconstruction, concussion, stress fracture, surgical repair — return to full play should follow a graded protocol with objective markers, not a calendar.

For ACL reconstruction specifically, the modern standard:

  • Minimum 9–12 months from surgery before unrestricted return to play, with most evidence supporting closer to 12 months for adolescent female athletes.
  • Functional testing — single-leg hop battery, isokinetic strength testing — at the end of rehab to confirm objective readiness.
  • Continued neuromuscular training (the same FIFA 11+ / PEP work) in perpetuity. Re-tear rates within two years of return are non-trivial; the protective effect of ongoing prevention work is real.

"Return to play" and "return to performance" are different milestones. The player can train safely well before she will feel like herself in games. Manage expectations accordingly.
