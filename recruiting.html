Chapter 02

The College Recruiting Roadmap

The contact rules changed in 2018, NIL changed everything in 2021, and the transfer portal changed everything again. Here is how college recruiting in women's soccer actually works now — without the panic.

For two decades, women's college soccer recruiting was a scramble that started embarrassingly young. Top programs were verbal-committing 14-year-olds. Then, in 2018, the NCAA passed a sweeping rule that pushed off-campus recruiting contact, official visits, and verbal offers to June 15 after sophomore year. The world it created is the world we live in now: a recruiting calendar that's later, more humane, and built around a small handful of pivot points.

This is what they look like, what they reward, and what to ignore.

1. The college landscape

"Going D1" is one possible outcome and not the only good one. Women's college soccer is played across five sanctioning bodies. Each has different scholarship rules, recruiting calendars, and academic profiles.

LevelProgramsAthletic aidNotes
NCAA Division I ~350 No sport-specific scholarship cap (House settlement, effective July 1, 2025). Roster limit of 28 women's soccer players. Schools may now scholarship every roster player, subject to a school-wide athletics revenue-share cap. Highest level of competition. Power-conference programs are the most competitive recruiting environment in women's college sports. The post-House landscape is more scholarship-rich on paper but rosters are smaller.
NCAA Division II ~270 Up to 9.9 scholarships (D2 retained sport-specific limits) Often combines athletic and academic aid into competitive packages. Strong soccer in many regions.
NCAA Division III ~440+ No athletic scholarships Excellent academics common. Aid is need- and merit-based. The most overlooked competitive option.
NAIA ~200 Up to 12 scholarships Smaller schools, often religiously affiliated. Looser recruiting rules. Honest pathway for late bloomers.
NJCAA (JUCO) ~190 (across divisions) Varies by division Two-year colleges. A real route for academic recovery, late development, or a stepping stone to a four-year program.

The number that surprises people

Approximately 7% of high-school girls' soccer players go on to play in the NCAA across all three divisions. Roughly 2% play Division I. Across all four-year college options including NAIA and JUCO, somewhere in the 9–12% range continue. There is much more college soccer in America than there is "Division I."

2. The contact rules, simplified

For NCAA Division I and II women's soccer, the timeline is built around two dates that have shaped recruiting since 2018:

  • Through sophomore year: No off-campus contact, no recruiting communication initiated by college coaches, no official visits, no verbal offers. Players can still email coaches. Coaches can confirm receipt and provide camp info, but cannot recruit.
  • June 15 after sophomore year: The dam breaks. Coaches may begin all forms of recruiting communication. Phone calls, emails, off-campus visits, official offers, verbal commitments, all start here.
  • September 1 of junior year: Some forms of contact (varies by sport and division) become permitted; in women's soccer the bigger gate is June 15.
  • Junior year through senior year: Official visits (paid by the school), early signing period (early November of senior year), and the regular signing window through spring.

Division III, NAIA, and JUCO have looser recruiting rules. Coaches at those levels can contact players essentially any time, and the practical recruiting calendar runs later (often well into senior year).

NCAA rules change periodically. We list the current state at Resources, but verify with the program's compliance office before any consequential decision.

3. A year-by-year timeline

Middle school (U12–U14)

Play, train, do school

You should not be doing recruiting work yet. Build a strong club foundation, take care of injuries, get your grades. The most important "recruiting" task at this age is learning to love training.

Freshman year (U15)

Start a list, attend camps for fun

Begin a working list of 25–40 schools that interest the player academically first, athletically second. Attend an ID camp or two — for the experience and to be seen, not to chase an offer. Update an early highlight reel. Keep grades high.

Sophomore year (U16)

The runway

Refine the list. Email coaches at target schools (introductions, schedules, video). Coaches can't reply with recruiting content yet but they read every email. Take the PSAT. Identify the showcases the player will attend post-June 15.

June 15 after sophomore year

The opening bell (D1/D2)

Coaches may now contact recruits. Expect emails, phone calls, and invitations to unofficial and official visits at programs you've already engaged. The work you did sophomore year is what generates June 15 conversations.

Junior year (U17)

Visits, offers, decisions

The bulk of D1 women's soccer recruiting now happens in junior year. Unofficial and official visits, offers (verbal or written), and many commitments. Take the SAT/ACT. Continue to refine the school list to a realistic top 5–10.

Senior year (U18)

Sign, or run the next-tier process

Early Signing Period opens in early November. Players with offers sign National Letters of Intent (D1/D2) or written commitments (D3, NAIA). For players still recruiting, senior year is when D3, NAIA, and many DII programs do their primary work.

4. How girls actually get scouted

College coaches identify recruits through, in roughly descending order:

  1. Club-league showcases. ECNL and the Girls Academy run dedicated college showcase events with hundreds of coaches in attendance. (GA's calendar increasingly includes joint events with MLS Next under the two leagues' 2024 strategic alliance.) This is the modal way Division I rosters are built.
  2. The player's club coach. Established club coaches have relationships with college programs. A short, honest email from a club coach saying "she's a fit for you" matters more than any highlight reel.
  3. ID camps. See below.
  4. Direct outreach from the player. Email + video + targeted research. Decisive at the D2/D3/NAIA levels and often the entry point at D1 too.
  5. Recruiting services and platforms (NCSA, SportsRecruits, etc.). Useful for organization and database access; not a substitute for the four above.

5. ID camps: useful, not magical

Programs run their own ID camps, charging $200–$500 per day. These are revenue events, but they are also genuinely how coaches sometimes find players. The honest framing:

  • Go to camps at programs that already know you. If you've emailed and they responded with camp info, that's a meaningful invitation.
  • Don't go to camps as a substitute for being at showcases. A good club showcase outdraws ten ID camps.
  • Bring a parent who is calm. The camp is a 90-minute audition; the parent can ruin it in the parking lot afterward.
  • Use camps to evaluate the program, too. You're seeing the real coaches, the real environment, and the actual culture. That cuts both ways.

6. Video, profiles, and the email

Three artifacts matter and only three: a highlight video, a profile page (or PDF), and an introductory email.

The highlight video

  • 3–5 minutes total. The first 60 seconds must include 8–12 of the best clips. Coaches stop watching before clip 5 if they're not interested.
  • Use a spotlight or arrow to identify the player at the start of every clip.
  • Show position-relevant work. A center back's video shows defending under pressure, line management, distribution. A forward's shows finishing, runs, and 1v1.
  • Include full game film links — coaches who like the highlight reel will ask for full games.
  • Update once per major showcase, not after every game.

The profile

One page. Name, graduation year, position, club, jersey #, height, GPA, test scores, club coach contact, schedule of upcoming events, and a video link. Resist the urge to design the profile of a CEO; coaches scan in 20 seconds.

The email

A model that works:

Coach Last Name —
I'm a ['28] center mid at [Club Name] in [Region], and I've been following [Program] for two seasons. I'd love to be on your radar. Below is my profile, video, and upcoming schedule. I'll be at [Showcase Name, Field 6, Sat 10:00am, jersey #14] if you have a chance to see me. I have a strong interest in your [program/major/conference] and would welcome the chance to learn more.

[Player Name] · [grad year] · [position]
Profile · Video · GPA · Coach contact

Personalize the second sentence. Cut every other word.

7. What an "offer" actually is

Most D1 women's soccer offers are verbal until a National Letter of Intent is signed senior year. Verbal commitments are non-binding by either party, and history shows they sometimes break. A few realities:

  • Coaches lose jobs. If the head coach who recruited you leaves, the next coach is not bound to honor the offer. A verbal commitment to a program is implicitly a commitment to a coach.
  • Money is rarely settled at verbal. An offer typically becomes a percentage when the financial-aid conversation gets specific. Ask for the percentage in writing before signing.
  • The transfer portal exists. Modern recruiting has to assume that some part of the team will turn over via portal each year. Coaches recruit accordingly. Players should plan accordingly.

8. Scholarships, NIL, and the money picture

The college-money picture changed substantially in 2025. The House v. NCAA settlement, approved in 2025 and effective July 1, eliminated sport-specific scholarship caps in NCAA Division I. Where women's soccer programs were previously limited to 14 athletic scholarships divided across the roster, programs now operate against a 28-player roster limit and may, in principle, offer scholarships to every player on it. The catch: each Division I school operates against a single annual athletics revenue-share cap (initially $20.5 million, growing roughly 4% per year), shared across all sports. Football and men's basketball typically claim the largest share, so the practical reality for most women's-soccer recruits is more athletic aid available than under the old 14-scholarship rule, but partial aid remains the norm and full rides are still concentrated at top programs.

Two further notes specific to the post-settlement era. First, "designated student-athletes" — players already on rosters as of April 7, 2025, or with a roster spot promised before that date — are protected and don't count against new roster limits during a transition period; ask programs you're recruiting to about their current roster math. Second, Division II, Division III, and NAIA were not bound by the same settlement and largely retain their prior scholarship structures.

For most families, the realistic financial path combines:

  • Athletic aid (partial), if any.
  • Academic / merit aid. At many DI, DII, and especially DIII institutions, academic aid is the larger lever. Strong grades and test scores increase the school's ability to construct a package.
  • Need-based aid (FAFSA / CSS Profile). Especially decisive at private universities and at DIII.
  • NIL (Name, Image, Likeness). Since 2021, college athletes — including women's soccer players — can be paid for personal brand, sponsorships, and appearances. NIL collectives at marquee schools have begun to function as de facto rosters payments at the top of the sport. NIL's influence at the women's-soccer level is real but uneven, and rules continue to evolve.

For a realistic discussion of total cost vs. aid by school type, see the Costs chapter.

9. Academics, eligibility, the rest of life

Two operational notes:

  • NCAA Eligibility Center. Any player intending to compete at the NCAA D1 or D2 level needs to register with the NCAA Eligibility Center, typically junior year. The Center certifies academic eligibility against core-course requirements and reviews amateurism status.
  • Academics are the lever. A strong GPA opens schools that athletic ability alone doesn't. The same player at a 3.9 has a substantially different recruiting list than at a 3.2.

10. The myths to retire

  • "You have to commit early." The opposite is now true at D1. June 15 after sophomore year is the floor; junior year is the meat of the calendar; some D1 commitments happen senior year.
  • "D1 is the only real college soccer." D3, NAIA, and the better D2 programs play very good soccer. The U.S. women's national team has, historically, drawn from across the divisions.
  • "My daughter has to be on a club's first team to be recruited." Coaches recruit individuals, not teams. Showing up at the right showcases on the right team matters; being the marquee player on a "lower" team often beats riding pine on a "top" one.
  • "Recruiting services will get her recruited." They will help her organize. They will not replace the four real channels in §4.
  • "Verbal commitments are binding." They are not, by either party. They are a strong intent, not a contract.
  • "More highlights = more interest." Better highlights = more interest. Three minutes of high-quality clips beats nine minutes of medium ones.

The sentence we'd tape to the fridge

Recruit the player to a school she'd want to attend if she weren't playing soccer. Soccer ends, sometimes earlier than planned. The school doesn't.
