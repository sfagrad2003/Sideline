Chapter 00 · New to the game

The Game, Explained

Soccer in plain English. The object, the field, the rules, the rhythm of a match, and the vocabulary you'll hear on the sideline. No prior knowledge required.

If you've shown up to your first practice, your first game, or your first parents' meeting and felt like everyone else has been at this for years, this chapter is for you. Soccer's rules are simpler than they look once you've seen one game. The vocabulary catches up. Within a season you'll be the parent in the folding chair calmly explaining offside to someone else. Here's everything you actually need to know to follow along.

1. The object of the game

Two teams of eleven players each try to put the ball into the other team's goal more times than the other team does the same to them. The team with more goals at the end of the match wins. If the goals are equal, the match is a tie (in regular-season play; in tournaments and playoffs there are extra periods or penalty kicks to decide a winner).

The single most important rule of soccer is the one that makes it different from almost every other sport: only the goalkeeper may use her hands, and only inside her own penalty area. The other ten players use feet, thighs, chest, and head — but not hands or arms.

2. The field, decoded

The field — sometimes called the pitch — is a rectangle of grass (or turf) with white lines marking specific areas. Knowing what those lines and zones mean makes the game much easier to watch.

The lines that matter most

The halfway line divides the field. The penalty area (the big rectangle around each goal) is where the goalkeeper may use her hands and where most fouls become penalty kicks. The goal area (the smaller rectangle inside it) is mostly for goal kicks. The touchlines are the long sides; cross them and play stops for a throw-in. The goal lines are the short ends; cross them and play stops for either a goal kick, a corner kick, or a goal.

3. How a match is structured

An adult or older-youth match is divided into two halves of 45 minutes, with a 15-minute halftime. Two key things to know about that clock:

  • The clock runs continuously. Unlike American football or basketball, the clock does not stop for fouls, throw-ins, or substitutions. Play moves quickly.
  • Stoppage time (added time, "injury time"). At the end of each half the referee adds extra minutes to make up for time lost to injuries, substitutions, and other delays. You'll see the fourth official hold up a board: "+3" means three minutes will be added. The clock keeps going past 45:00; the half ends when the referee whistles, usually at the next dead ball after the added time has elapsed.

If the score is tied at the end of regulation:

  • Regular-season league games typically just end as a tie.
  • Tournaments and playoffs usually have two 15-minute extra-time periods. If still tied, the match is decided by a penalty-kick shootout: each team takes five alternating penalty kicks; if still tied, sudden-death kicks until one team scores and the other doesn't.

4. The players (and substitutes)

Each team has eleven players on the field, including exactly one goalkeeper. Each team also has substitutes on the bench.

  • Goalkeeper: Wears a different-colored jersey from the rest of her team. Only player allowed to use hands, and only within her own penalty area.
  • The other ten: Field players. They take up positions across defense, midfield, and attack. The classical 1–11 numbering and a 4-3-3 diagram are in the next section.
  • Substitutes. Most adult professional matches allow up to five substitutes per team. Youth and college rules vary — many youth leagues allow unlimited substitutions, and a player who comes off can typically come back on later in the match. (Professional and adult amateur substitutions are usually permanent.)

If a team has a player sent off (a red card — see §7), they must continue with one fewer player. They cannot replace her. A team reduced to seven players abandons the match.

5. The eleven numbers & the three thirds

You'll hear coaches and parents call players by number — "the 9 is having a great day" or "we need a real 6." That language comes from the traditional jersey numbering of association football, where each shirt number signaled a position on the field. Modern teams hand out any squad number a player wants (you'll see #1s playing in midfield), but the positional numbers still rule the conversation.

The three thirds

The field is informally divided into three equal horizontal bands. The lines aren't actually painted on the grass, but coaches use them constantly to teach: "We don't lose the ball cheaply in our defending third." "We win it back in the middle third." "We're patient in the attacking third." If you remember nothing else from this section, remember the thirds — they are how everyone in the sport thinks about space.

  • The defending third is your own goal end. The first job here is to not concede. Mistakes get punished. Goalkeeper, back four, and the 6 do most of their work in this zone.
  • The middle third is the contest zone. Most of the game's possession, transitions, and pressing happen here. Midfielders dominate; fullbacks and the 6 connect with the front three.
  • The attacking third is the opponent's goal end. Decisions here are about creating chances: combinations, crosses, runs in behind, finishing. Wingers, the 9, and the 10 do their best work here.

6. How formations work

A "formation" describes how a team's ten outfield players (everyone except the goalkeeper) are arranged at kick-off. It's written as a string of numbers, listed from defense to attack:

  • 4-3-3 means 4 defenders, 3 midfielders, 3 forwards.
  • 4-4-2 means 4 defenders, 4 midfielders, 2 forwards.
  • 4-2-3-1 means 4 defenders, 2 holding mids, 3 attacking mids, 1 striker.

The numbers always add to 10 — the goalkeeper is implied. Subtle differences in how those numbers are organized — flat midfield versus diamond, single pivot versus double pivot, "inverted" wingers versus traditional ones — get talked about in long detail by coaches but rarely matter to a new fan watching her first season.

The formations you'll see in girls' soccer

FormationWhere you'll see itWhat it's good at
4-3-3 The most common shape at modern U.S. youth (U13+) and across women's pro and college play. The U.S. Women's National Team has used variants of it for much of the last decade. Width through wingers, central density through three midfielders, balanced defense. Punishes opponents who don't defend the wide channels.
4-4-2 The classic American formation. Still common in U.S. high school and recreational youth. Less common at the elite club level today. Simple to teach, strong central defense, two strikers create chaos in the box. Vulnerable in midfield against a 3-mid system.
4-2-3-1 A sophisticated modern setup, common in pro soccer and increasingly in elite girls' clubs. Two holding mids give defensive stability; three attacking mids behind a lone striker create overloads in the final third.
3-5-2 / 3-4-3 Three-back systems with wing-backs. Less common at U.S. youth; popular with certain pro teams. Numerical advantage in midfield; wing-backs create width. Demands very high-fitness wide players.
Small-sided shapes At U6–U10 you'll see 4v4 and 7v7. Common shapes are 1-2-1 (a "diamond") in 4v4 and 2-3-1 in 7v7. Built for development at the right age, not for tactical sophistication. Touches and decisions per minute are the goal.

Formations are starting shapes, not jobs

The single most important thing to know about formations is that they describe a team's starting arrangement, not where players spend the game. In modern soccer, players move constantly. A 4-3-3 might look like a 4-4-2 when defending and a 2-3-5 in possession. Outside backs become wingers; wingers tuck in to become midfielders; the 6 drops between the center backs to make a back three. By the time the ball has been in play for ninety seconds, the formation on paper has bent and reshaped itself a dozen times.

What the formation actually communicates is the balance of priorities the coach has chosen: how many players she wants near the ball when defending, how many higher up the field when attacking, and which channels — central, wide, the half-spaces in between — get extra bodies. It's a starting picture and a coaching language. It is not a cage.

The math of formations

Sometimes you'll see a formation written with four numbers (4-2-3-1) or even five (3-1-4-2). Add them up; they should reach 10. If you see "4-4-2 diamond" versus "flat 4-4-2," that describes the shape of the midfield: a diamond has a defensive mid at the back, an attacking mid at the front, and two wide mids; a flat 4-4-2 has all four midfielders side by side.

7. Starting and restarting play

Soccer is a continuous game; the action only stops when the ball goes out of play, when a goal is scored, or when the referee blows the whistle for a foul or other reason. Each restart has its own rule.

SituationWhat happens
Kick-off Used to start each half and to restart play after a goal. Taken from the center spot. Both teams must be on their own half until the ball is touched.
Throw-in When the ball crosses a touchline (sideline). The team that did not touch it last throws it back in. Two hands, both feet on the ground, the ball delivered from over the head.
Goal kick When the attacking team puts the ball over the defending goal line (without scoring). Restart with a kick from inside the goal area, taken by anyone (usually the goalkeeper).
Corner kick When the defending team puts the ball over their own goal line (without scoring). The attacking team gets to take a kick from the nearest corner arc. A real chance to score.
Free kick Awarded for fouls (see §7). Direct free kicks can be shot directly into the goal; indirect free kicks must touch a second player first. The opposing team must stand at least 10 yards (about 9 meters) away.
Penalty kick For a foul committed by the defending team inside its own penalty area. Taken from the penalty spot, 12 yards from goal, with only the goalkeeper between the kicker and the goal. See §8.
Drop ball Used in some situations where play stops for a non-foul reason (e.g., outside interference). Referee drops the ball to a player from the team that last touched it.

8. Offside, the famous rule

The most-asked-about rule in soccer. The good news: it's actually pretty simple.

An attacking player is in an offside position if, at the moment a teammate plays the ball, she is:

  1. In the opponent's half of the field, AND
  2. Closer to the opponent's goal line than both the ball and the second-to-last opponent (which usually means: closer to the goal than the last defender, since the goalkeeper is typically the last one).

Being in an offside position is not, by itself, a foul. The player is only penalized if she becomes actively involved in play — receives the pass, interferes with an opponent, or gains an advantage from being there.

A few things that are not offside:

  • Being level with the second-to-last defender (level is onside).
  • Being in your own half of the field.
  • Receiving the ball directly from a goal kick, a throw-in, or a corner kick.

Why the rule exists

Without an offside rule, you could have an attacker just standing next to the opposing goalkeeper waiting for a long ball. Offside forces attackers to time their runs and stay connected with the play, which is what makes soccer's attacking patterns interesting to watch.

9. Fouls, free kicks & cards

A foul is an illegal action against an opponent. The most common ones are kicking, tripping, pushing, holding, or charging recklessly. A foul stops play and gives a free kick to the opposing team from the spot of the foul (or a penalty kick if it happened in the penalty area).

For more serious offenses, the referee shows the offending player a card:

  • Yellow card. A formal warning — sometimes called being "booked" or "cautioned." For reckless play, persistent fouling, dissent, time-wasting, or unsporting behavior. The player stays on the field but knows that one more yellow means a red.
  • Red card. The player is sent off and the team plays the rest of the match a player short. A red is shown for: a second yellow card; serious foul play; violent conduct; spitting; denying an obvious goal-scoring opportunity (e.g., a defender deliberately handling a goal-bound ball); or using offensive language.

The handball rule deserves a paragraph because it is the most argued-about call in soccer. In short: a player commits a foul if she deliberately handles the ball, OR if her arm is in an "unnaturally large" position and the ball strikes it (e.g., raised above the shoulder). The arm being naturally at the side is generally not a foul, even if the ball hits it. Recent rule clarifications have tried to make this more consistent; in practice it remains a judgment call.

10. Penalty kicks

Awarded for a foul committed by the defending team inside their own penalty area. The biggest single chance in soccer.

  • The ball is placed on the penalty spot, 12 yards from the goal line.
  • One attacker takes the kick. The goalkeeper must remain on the goal line until the kick is taken. All other players from both teams stand outside the penalty area and outside the penalty arc until the ball is struck.
  • The kicker may not touch the ball a second time until another player has touched it.
  • If the goalkeeper saves it (or the kick is missed) and the ball stays in play, anyone may follow up.

Conversion rate at the elite level is roughly 75–80%. They are not gimmes, but they are the most likely scoring opportunity in soccer.

11. The flow of a typical match

An average game has 8–14 shots per team, 1–3 goals per side, and roughly 25 stoppages of 5–10 seconds each. Possession swings constantly. Some patterns to listen for from the sideline or commentary booth:

  • "They're keeping the ball." Possession-based teams pass to control tempo. The team without the ball tries to disrupt, press, and force a turnover.
  • "Counter-attack." Winning the ball and immediately attacking forward at speed, before the opponent can reorganize.
  • "Set piece." Any restart from a stationary ball — corner kicks, free kicks, throw-ins. About 25–30% of goals come from set pieces, so they're more important than they look.
  • "Through ball." A pass that splits two defenders and arrives in the space behind them, for an attacker running onto it.
  • "Switching the field." A long pass from one side of the field to the other, often used to find space when the defense is loaded up on one side.
  • "Pressing." Defending high up the field, with multiple players collectively closing down the ball-carrier rather than dropping back.

12. A note on youth-soccer rules

Almost everything above describes adult and older-youth soccer. Youth rules differ to keep the game age-appropriate. The most important differences in U.S. youth soccer:

AgeFormatMatch lengthKey differences
U6/U74v4 (no goalkeeper)~32 min total, four short quartersNo throw-ins (kick-ins instead), no offside, smaller ball (size 3).
U8/U94v4 or 7v7 (with GK from U9)~40–48 min, halves or quartersLimited offside, smaller field, smaller ball.
U107v7~50 minBuild-out line introduced (defenders drop back when GK has the ball, to encourage playing out from the back). Heading not allowed in games.

U13+11v11~70–90 minFull FIFA rules; size 5 ball; heading allowed.

Substitution rules in youth and high-school soccer are also looser — most leagues allow players to come on and off multiple times during a match. See the Health & Safety chapter for details on heading guidelines and concussion protocols.

13. The vocabulary, fast

Words you'll hear from coaches, commentators, and the parents in the next folding chair.

  • Pitch: the field.
  • Kit: the uniform. Jersey, shorts, socks.
  • Boots: cleats.
  • Match / fixture: a game.
  • Half: the two periods of play. "First half," "second half."
  • Stoppage time / added time / injury time: the minutes added on at the end of each half.
  • Clean sheet: a game where a goalkeeper / defense doesn't concede a goal.
  • Brace: two goals by one player. Hat-trick: three goals by one player in one match.
  • Assist: the pass that directly leads to a goal.
  • Cap: an appearance for a national team. "She has 50 caps."
  • Friendly: a non-competitive match (often used by national teams).
  • Side: a team. "The U.S. side starts at 4."
  • Nutmeg ("meg"): when a player puts the ball through an opponent's legs and collects it on the other side. Brings cheers.
  • Box-to-box: a midfielder who runs from her own penalty box to the opponent's, defending and attacking equally.
  • Through ball: a pass into space behind defenders for a runner.
  • Switch: a long pass across the field to the opposite side.
  • Cross: a pass from the wing into the penalty area.
  • 1v1: a one-on-one situation.
  • Set piece: a restart from a stationary ball — corner, free kick, etc.
  • The 18: the penalty area (18 yards out from goal).
  • The 6: the goal area (6 yards out from goal). Also "the six-yard box."
  • The D: the penalty arc just outside the penalty area.
  • Touchline / sideline: the long sides of the field.
  • Off the line: a defender clearing a goal-bound ball that has crossed in front of the goalkeeper.
  • Park the bus: a defensive tactic of putting almost everyone behind the ball to protect a lead.
