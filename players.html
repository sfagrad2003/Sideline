Chapter 06 · For players

The Player's Playbook

Positions, decisions, training that actually works, the goalkeeper question, and how to last. Written for you — not your parents.

If you're reading this, somebody else probably opened the page first. That's fine. The rest of this site is full of useful stuff for adults. This chapter is for you. We are going to skip the inspirational posters and talk about the actual game — what the position you play asks of you, what to train when nobody's watching, how to handle the recruiting process if you go that direction, and how to keep loving this when it gets hard.

1. How the game actually works

Most players are taught soccer as a sequence of touches. The better players think of it as a sequence of decisions. The ball arrives, but before it arrives you've already chosen a body shape, a first touch direction, and the pass after that. The player you watch and think "she has so much time" doesn't actually have more time than you do; she has decided earlier.

If you take one thing from this page, take this: play the picture before the ball. Lift your head every two seconds. Know where your nearest teammate is, your nearest opponent, and the open space between them. The first touch is the answer to a question you've already asked.

2. Positions, in plain English

Below: the field, the three thirds, and the eleven players in a 4-3-3 with traditional numbering. Then a more honest description of each position.

The thirds, in plain English

Defending third is your own goal end — the first job there is to not concede. Middle third is the contest zone where most possession, pressing, and transitions happen. Attacking third is the opponent's goal end, where decisions are about creating chances: combinations, crosses, runs in behind, finishing.

Center back

You read the game backward. You see what's about to break down before it does. The technical floor for a center back has risen — modern center backs are expected to play out of pressure, drive into midfield, and switch the field. The mental requirements are higher: communication, responsibility for the back line, and the ability to recover from your own mistake without folding.

Outside back

The fitness position. You're expected to defend like a back and attack like a winger, in the same minute. Modern outside backs are often the most fit player on the field. Strong outside backs are recruited heavily because they're so hard to find at the elite level.

Defensive midfielder (the 6)

The position that decides games and gets the least credit. You shield the back line, win the second ball, and start attacks. Vision and composure matter more than top-end speed. If you grow up to be the player coaches trust most, this is often the position.

Central midfielder (8/10)

The connector. You play forward, you turn, you arrive late in the box. The 10 is creator-first; the 8 is shuttler-first. The expectation now is that all central mids defend.

Winger

1v1 problem-solver. Your value is creating chances — for yourself, by going at fullbacks, and for others, with crosses or cutbacks. The modern winger needs to be comfortable in tight spaces and unselfish in front of goal.

Forward

The position with the most volatile reputation and the lowest tolerance for off-days. Movement is the whole job: when to check, when to spin, when to stay. Finishing is repeatable; first-touch composure under contact is rarer and more valuable.

Goalkeeper

See below. It's its own world.

3. The decision-making thing

The single most useful drill at any level: scanning. Lift your head every two seconds. Specifically:

  • Where is space?
  • Where is pressure coming from?
  • Where are my closest teammates? Are they receivers, runners, or in support?

Every elite player you've ever watched is doing this. Some are doing it 10+ times before a possession. The good news: you can practice this in any practice, any game, any rondo, by training the habit of looking up before the ball arrives.

4. Training that actually moves you

Two facts that are uncomfortable but true:

  1. The biggest gains in your game come from more reps with decisions, not more reps without decisions. 1,000 wall-passes against a wall makes you good at wall-passes; small-sided games make you good at soccer.
  2. You will get better faster from playing pickup or 2v2 with friends than from another hour of cone work.

That said, individual technical work isn't useless. The honest version of "what to do on your own":

  • Both feet, every session. Most youth players have one foot. Most elite players have two. The difference between you at 16 and the player you wish you were is largely on the side of you you don't currently use.
  • Juggling and ball mastery for first-touch quality. 100 a day, both feet, is plenty.
  • Wall passing with two-touch (in to one foot, out from the other). Hundreds of reps per session, varying angles.
  • Striking technique: 50 strikes from outside the 18, working laces and inside-of-foot, both feet. Recording your form on a phone helps more than you'd think.
  • Plyometrics and landing mechanics — read the safety chapter. The same routine that prevents ACL injuries will make you faster.
  • Conditioning that resembles the game — short, repeated, max-effort sprints with full recovery, not long jogs.

The single highest-leverage piece of off-field work for a 14–18 year-old is sleep. Adolescents need 8–10 hours; chronically tired players are slower, more injury-prone, and worse decision-makers. Your phone after 9 p.m. is your enemy.

5. The goalkeeper section

If you're a goalkeeper, almost everything written about field players applies less to you, and the volume of position-specific writing for goalkeepers is roughly nothing. Some quick truth:

  • Goalkeeper is a separate sport played on the same field. Pick a club that has a real goalkeeper coach with regular GK-specific training, ideally weekly.
  • Footwork is the foundation. Hands matter, but feet make hands work.
  • Distribution has become the differentiator. Modern goalkeepers play with the ball at their feet under pressure. Train it.
  • The mental game is enormous. Goalkeepers carry mistakes in a way field players don't. Develop a routine to reset after a goal — a phrase, a breath, a glove tap. The best keepers in the world have one.
  • You will need fewer numbers and more reps than the team prescribes. Find the extra session.
  • Goalkeeping is recruited differently. College programs typically recruit 1–2 goalkeepers per class; the timeline can be later, the bar higher, the market smaller. Plan accordingly.

6. Recruiting yourself

This section assumes you want to play in college. If you don't — or you're not sure — that's fine. Skip this and read section 7. If you do, read the recruiting chapter first; this is the player-facing version.

Three things to own as a player

  1. Your school list. 25–40 schools you'd actually want to attend, ordered by academic fit first. Use the school's own admissions website, not anyone else's. If you wouldn't be happy at the school without soccer, take it off the list.
  2. Your video. 3–5 minutes. Best clips first. Updated after each major showcase. Don't add music; coaches mute it.
  3. Your email. One short email per program. Personalize the second sentence. Include profile, video, and where you'll be (showcase, field, time, jersey #) in the next 60 days.

What you don't need

An expensive recruiting service. They organize the work; they don't do it. The actual work is yours.

The hardest part

Coaches won't always answer your emails. That's not personal. Keep emailing. Keep updating. Coaches you didn't expect will show up. Coaches you really wanted will go quiet. The college recruiting process is a numbers game with a small denominator and a lot of luck. Treat it as a process, not a verdict.

7. How to last

Soccer ends, eventually. For some it ends at 18, for some at 22, for some at 35. The way you play and the way you live in the sport now is what determines whether you make it to that ending feeling proud, or just relieved. Some honest things from older players:

  • Your body is the asset. Stretch, sleep, eat enough, talk about pain. Play with people who do the same. The safety chapter is for you, not your parents.
  • Have a sport beyond this one. Run, lift, surf, climb, anything. Players who play multiple sports through their teens last longer in their primary one.
  • Tell someone if it's too much. Coach, parent, sibling, therapist. The athletes who burn out are not the ones who said something. They're the ones who didn't.
  • The team is the joy. Even when the games are bad. Find the teammates who love it; protect that.
  • The college decision is yours. Listen to advice, but the choice is yours.
  • If you stop loving it, that's information. Information is not failure.

One last thing

The version of you you'd most want to grow into is not the one who scored the most goals. It's the one who is still playing pickup at 30 because soccer is part of who she is. Train and play for that person. Everyone else's version of "successful" is somebody else's version.
