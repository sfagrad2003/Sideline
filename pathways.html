Chapter 01

Pathways & Levels of Play

From Saturday-morning rec at the local park to a roster spot in ECNL or the Girls Academy — a translator for every tier of American youth girls soccer, and how a player actually moves between them.

If you've heard a parent in a folding chair use the words "ECNL," "GA," "ECRL," "ODP," and "MLS Next alliance" in a single breath and felt your eyes glaze over, you are not alone. American youth soccer has, over the last fifteen years, evolved from a relatively flat pyramid into something more like a system of interconnected leagues, each with its own logo, calendar, and political history. The good news: under the alphabet, the structure is actually pretty navigable.

This chapter walks the pyramid from the bottom up — what each level is for, who it's for, and the realistic moves between them.

1. Recreational soccer

Almost every American girl who plays soccer starts in rec. Rec leagues are run by towns, YMCAs, and small nonprofits, organized loosely under U.S. Youth Soccer's state associations. Practices are once or twice a week. Games are local. Kids of mixed ability play together; trophies are participation; uniforms are screen-printed t-shirts; the snack rotation is sacred.

Rec is where children fall in love with the sport. It is also the level most underrated by ambitious families, because it does the developmental work that no curriculum will ever match: thousands of touches per practice in small-sided chaos, with adults who mostly stay out of the way.

The youngest ages

For ages roughly 4–8, U.S. Soccer's age-appropriate guidance is small-sided everything: 4v4 with no goalkeeper at U6/U7, then 7v7 with keepers at U9/U10. If a "select" or "premier" team is being pitched to your six-year-old, you are looking at marketing, not development.

2. Travel / competitive

"Travel" is the loose umbrella for the next step up: tryout-based teams that play other teams from a wider geography. In the U.S. Youth Soccer (USYS) world, this means leagues run by state associations and regional bodies — often called Premier, Classic, Elite Premier, or similar within the state. The Eastern Development Program (EDP) is a large example in the East; in the West, state leagues fill the same role.

Travel is where the calendar starts to matter. Fall and Spring "seasons" become real, with state cups in the spring, plus tournaments scattered through holiday weekends. Most travel teams practice 2–3 times a week and play 8–14 league games per season, plus tournaments.

3. "Club" soccer, defined

Almost every team above rec is technically a "club" team — that is, run by a private soccer club rather than a town. But in everyday usage, when people say their daughter plays "club," they usually mean a team that plays in one of the structured national or regional league systems described next. The club is the organization (e.g., a non-profit youth soccer organization in your area). The league is the competition that the club's teams enter.

A single club might run, on the girls' side, an ECNL team, an ECNL Regional League team, a GA team, several state-league teams, and an academy/development program for younger ages. Bigger clubs have hundreds of girls. Smaller clubs may have one team per age group.

4. The elite national leagues

Above the state-league layer sit two national elite leagues on the girls' side. They are not officially "ranked" against each other, and the strongest team in each varies by region and age group. These are the leagues college coaches scout most heavily.

ECNL Girls

The Elite Clubs National League (ECNL) was founded as a girls' league in 2009 as a breakaway, club-driven competition. (A boys' ECNL was added in 2017.) On the girls' side it is the most established elite league, with U13–U19 age groups, regional conferences across the country, a national playoff, and a top-tier Champions League playoff for the strongest qualifiers. ECNL is widely considered the most heavily scouted girls' league in the country and is where a large share of NCAA Division I players are identified. Member clubs are vetted; teams typically play 20–28 league matches plus showcase events.

Girls Academy (GA)

The Girls Academy League launched in 2020, after U.S. Soccer disbanded the Development Academy on the girls' side. GA was founded as a player-first, club-led alternative with a similar national structure: U13–U19, regional conferences across the country, national showcases, and playoffs. Many former DA clubs joined GA; many also joined ECNL. In practice, GA is competitive with ECNL in many markets, and a top GA team will look much like a top ECNL team to a college coach.

The MLS Next alliance

A point of frequent confusion: there is no "MLS Next Girls" league. MLS Next is Major League Soccer's youth competition, and as of 2025–26 it remains a boys-only platform. In December 2024, however, MLS Next announced a strategic alliance with the Girls Academy. The alliance covers joint coaching education, regional showcase events that include both leagues, integration of select GA clubs into MLS Next's signature Generation adidas Cup, and aligned standards across the boys' and girls' sides. So when you hear "MLS clubs and Girls Academy," that's what's being described — a partnership between MLS Next and GA, not a separate girls' league.

Which one matters more?

Less than the marketing implies. At the top end, the difference between an ECNL and a GA program is typically about which clubs in your geography are in which league, not the league name itself. The realistic question is "which of the best clubs in driving distance has a roster spot for my daughter, and which coach do we trust?"

5. The "second tier": ECRL, GA Aspire, NL, EDP

Each elite league runs a sister/regional level beneath it, and there are independent leagues that fill the same space. These are very legitimate levels of play — many state-cup champions and ID-camp invitees come from here.

  • ECNL Regional League (ECRL): A level below ECNL within ECNL clubs. Many strong programs use ECRL as a development pipeline; movement up to ECNL within the same club is common.
  • GA Aspire: The Girls Academy's official second tier, launched in February 2025 and operated in collaboration with the Development Player League (DPL). Adds a dedicated platform for GA clubs' second teams at U13–U19, with regional league play, regional and national showcases, and national championships at each age group. The longer-running DPL and DPL Open continue beneath that as additional development tiers.
  • USYS National League / NL Conferences / NLP: The U.S. Youth Soccer national platform, with multiple tiers (NL, NL Conferences, NL P.R.O.). Strong in many regions, particularly outside the big metros.
  • EDP, CJSL, NPL: Regional leagues such as the Eastern Development Program, plus National Premier League (NPL) divisions, which connect into USYS national pathways.

6. ODP and the YNT pool

Two separate identification pathways run alongside (not above) club soccer.

Olympic Development Program (ODP)

Run by U.S. Youth Soccer at the state and regional level, ODP is the longest-standing identification program in American youth soccer. Players try out for their state team, the strongest are invited to regional camps, and a handful are seen by U.S. national-team scouts. ODP's relative weight versus club leagues has decreased as ECNL and GA have grown, but it remains a meaningful avenue, especially for players outside the biggest clubs.

Youth National Team (YNT) pool

U.S. Soccer scouts and runs Talent Identification camps and Youth National Team (YNT) training events from roughly U14 up through U23. Inclusion is invitation-only and largely scouted from elite-league play and ID camps. The YNT pathway is, for the very small percentage of players it touches, the road to the senior national team.

7. High school soccer

High school soccer is its own animal. Run by state high-school athletic associations, the season varies — Fall in much of the country, Spring in the Northeast and parts of the West. Eligibility rules around playing simultaneously with elite-league teams have changed and continue to vary:

  • ECNL: Allows high school participation. Many ECNL clubs slow training during the high-school season.
  • Girls Academy: Allows high school participation, with some scheduling considerations for showcases.
  • MLS Next: A boys-only league as of 2025–26; not a consideration for girls' high school eligibility.

For most players, high school soccer is not the primary recruiting venue (college coaches scout club showcases), but it remains the most beloved soccer experience many girls will have — playing for the school, with friends, in front of the people you actually go to school with. A useful rule of thumb: high school is for the soul; club is for the résumé.

8. How players actually move up (and down)

The ladder is real, but it is not linear, and it is not deterministic. Real movement happens on these triggers:

Tryouts (May–June)

Annual tryouts at virtually every club

Most clubs hold open tryouts in May/June for the following season. Players try out for the highest team they realistically belong on; coaches place them where the player and roster fit. The lower team within the same club is the soft landing — not failure.

Mid-season call-up

Movement within the club

Strong clubs move players up to higher teams mid-year — for training, for games, or permanently. Healthy clubs treat this as routine. If your club guards its rosters jealously, that's a signal.

Cross-club moves

Switching clubs

Most state associations have a seasonal release window. Cross-club moves are common and not a moral failing. Two questions matter: (1) what's the player getting that she isn't getting now, and (2) what's the relationship cost?

U13–U15

The biggest sort

The largest level-of-play sorting tends to happen between U12 and U14. Bodies change, motivation diverges, and the elite-league teams form. This is also a high-injury window — see the Health & Safety chapter.

U16+

The recruiting filter

By U16, league/team is mostly a function of where a player wants to be seen by college coaches. Movement at this stage is strategic, not developmental.

9. How to evaluate a club

Brand is the loudest signal and not always the most important one. We'd ask the following, in roughly this order, before signing anywhere:

  • Who coaches the team she'd be on? Not the club's marquee coach, the actual one. Watch a training session.
  • What's the practice-to-game ratio? Healthy is around 3:1 (three training sessions per game). Game-heavy environments produce tired players, not better ones.
  • Are training sessions purposeful? Look for small-sided games, repetition with decision-making, and small coaching points — not lap-running and lectures.
  • How does the club handle injuries? Ask specifically about ACL prevention programs, return-to-play protocols, and access to athletic trainers.
  • How does the club treat its lower teams? A club that loves its top team and tolerates its second team is a club to skip.
  • How is communication with parents? Reasonable, honest, in writing — or vague and emotional? You will live with this for years.
  • What's the realistic college support if you'll need it? Ask for the prior class's commitment list and how it was actually built.
  • What does it cost — all-in? Player fees + tournaments + travel + uniforms + training extras. Get the real number. See Costs.

The thing nobody says out loud

The best club for a 10-year-old is rarely the best club for a 16-year-old, and the best club for a midfielder is rarely the same as for a goalkeeper. Plan for movement. The families who are happiest in this sport are the ones who make club choice a multi-year decision, not a religion.
