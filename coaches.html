Chapter 07 · For coaches

The Coach's Compass

Age-appropriate curriculum, modern training methods, building team culture, and the things coaching girls demands that the youth-coaching playbook of 1995 didn't get right.

There is no single more leveraged figure in a youth soccer player's life than her coach. The coach decides what training looks like, what the culture is, how injuries are managed, and — at the levels that matter for college recruiting — who is seen. This chapter is for the coach who wants to do that job well, and for the coach who is doing it well and looking for sharper language for what she already knows.

1. Age-appropriate development

The U.S. Soccer Player Development Initiatives (PDIs) and the broader youth-development consensus converge on a set of age-appropriate priorities. The framework below is a synthesis, intended as a working starting point.

AgeFormatPrimary focusWhat to avoid
U6–U84v4 (no GK at U6/U7)Ball familiarity, joy, lots of touches, freedom to playDrills with lines. Adult instruction during play. Selection.
U9–U107v7 with GK1v1, dribbling, passing in small groups, decision exposureHeading. Long balls. Tactical complexity.
U11–U129v9Combinations, receiving under pressure, position introductionSpecialization to a single position. Overuse.
U13–U1511v11Tactical foundations, position-specific work, conditioning that resembles the gameVolume during growth spurt. Identity narrowing too early.

2. What good sessions look like

A modern training session looks less like the calisthenics of 1990 and more like a sequence of game-like problems. Most sessions of any age share an architecture:

  1. Activation / injury-prevention warm-up (15–20 min). FIFA 11+, PEP, or equivalent — see the safety chapter. This is non-negotiable for adolescent female athletes.
  2. Technical-into-functional block (15–20 min). Isolated technique introduced, then immediately pressed into a game-like context. No 30-minute static passing patterns.
  3. Small-sided games or positional games (20–30 min). The bulk of the session. Real opponents, real decisions, real consequences. This is where players learn the sport.
  4. Conditioned scrimmage (15–25 min). Larger-sided play with a coaching constraint or theme. The closest thing to the actual game.
  5. Cool-down / mobility / closing (5–10 min). Brief, but consistent. A space for the team to land before going home.

The single biggest mistake in youth coaching is too much time on isolated technique and not enough on game-like decision-making. Players don't learn soccer by repeating one motion; they learn by repeating decisions in the presence of pressure.

The constraint-led approach

Modern coaching has moved toward "constraint-led" design — shaping the game to elicit the behavior you want, rather than telling players what to do. Want them to switch the field? Make a 4-goal game where you can only score by switching first. The constraint does the coaching.

3. Coaching girls — what's different, and what isn't

The serious answer is: less than the stereotypes suggest, and more than the egalitarian impulse wants to admit. The technical, tactical, and physical demands of the game don't care about gender. But the operating environment around the player has some real differences that good coaches account for.

What's actually different

  • Injury risk profile. Higher ACL risk, higher concussion rate. Read the safety chapter in full. Treat the warm-up as a non-negotiable, not a finishing-school addition.
  • The growth and puberty window hits younger and earlier than for boys, with bigger short-term effects on motor control. Plan training load and skill work accordingly through U13–U15.
  • Energy availability and the menstrual cycle are real performance and health variables. Many elite environments now openly discuss them and adjust training around them. Most youth environments don't. Closing that gap is a real lever.
  • Communication style under criticism. Generalizations are dangerous, but in our experience: many young female players respond more strongly than young male players to delivery of feedback, separate from its content. The same true thing said two different ways will land very differently. Skilled coaches adjust delivery without softening content.

What isn't different

  • The game. Don't dumb it down. Don't speak in different tactical vocabulary. Don't avoid challenging concepts.
  • The standard. Hold girls accountable. Coach hard. Demand quality. The coaches players remember most fondly are not the easiest; they're the ones who took them seriously.
  • The capacity for toughness, leadership, competitiveness, and grit. Don't assume what is or isn't there. Make space for it.

4. Building team culture

Culture is the set of behaviors that the team accepts when the coach isn't watching. It's built less by speeches than by a thousand small decisions about what is and isn't tolerated.

  • Establish team standards by week two. Punctuality, effort, language, kit, phones. Write them down. The team should own them by month two.
  • Address the culture problem the day it appears. Eye-rolling at the coach, snickering at a teammate's mistake, the "two captains and an outsider" group dynamic — name it the day you see it. The cost of avoidance compounds.
  • Pull the most popular player into the leadership conversation early. Whether or not she's the best player. Cultures are set by who's loudest in the locker room.
  • Allow disagreement. Players who feel they can disagree with the coach without consequence are players who will speak up about a teammate, an injury, or a problem.
  • Defend your worst player from your best one. Not on the field, but in the standards you enforce. Cultures collapse when the star is held to a different bar.
  • Mark transitions. A short ritual at the start of practice, the end of practice, and a one-sentence theme for the week. Repetition is what makes culture.

5. Load management and the season

You will be the most informed person in the room about training load, even if the parents have read more on the internet. Some practical principles:

  • Peak height velocity (the growth spurt) is the load-management window. A player adding 8 cm in a year is a different organism from the one she was last season. Reduce volume; emphasize quality and motor control.
  • The week is the unit, not the day. Don't program three high-intensity days in a row. Plan a week with intensity peaks and recovery troughs.
  • Tournaments are not normal. Three or four games in a weekend is, physiologically, a significant insult. Recover the following week. Don't make Tuesday a hard session.
  • The off-season is real. Schedule one. Two weeks completely off, plus a 4–6 week period of low-volume cross-training, every year. The body adapts during recovery.
  • Track who's tired. Subjective wellness check-ins (sleep, mood, soreness, motivation, 1–5) cost nothing and identify drift before it becomes injury.

6. Injury prevention as a coaching job

If you do nothing else as a result of this chapter, do this: run the FIFA 11+ or an equivalent neuromuscular protocol three times per week, every week, for the entire season. Most teams that adopt prevention warm-ups abandon them in week six. The teams that don't see meaningful reductions in serious injury.

The protocol is not optional, especially with female adolescent athletes during the U13–U15 window. The full case lives in the Health & Safety chapter; what's required of the coach:

  • Learn the protocol. Demonstrate it.
  • Run it as designed, not as a token jog.
  • Coach landing mechanics. "Soft, knees over toes, no inward collapse." Cue it in every plyometric drill.
  • Build hamstring strength. Nordic curls, RDLs, single-leg hinges, progressions appropriate to age.
  • Honor concussion protocol. If suspected, the player is done for the day. Period.
  • Listen when a player says she's hurt. The cost of one bad call is months of rehabilitation.

7. Working with parents

Parents are not the enemy. They are the operating environment. A few practical principles:

  • Set communication norms early. Email cadence, response time, what topics are appropriate, what's off-limits (lineup decisions; another player's playing time). Write them down.
  • The 24-hour rule. Ask parents not to email about a game within 24 hours of it. Adopt the same rule yourself.
  • Push player issues onto the player. By U13, your conversation about playing time is with the player, not the parent. Insist on it.
  • Honest is faster than nice. Tell parents what you see in the player, including hard things, with kindness. The parents most likely to become a problem are the ones with whom you avoided the honest conversation.
  • Anticipate the recruiting question. By U15, families want to know what their daughter's college pathway looks like. Have a real answer. If you don't, partner with someone who does.

8. Coach development

Coaching is a craft. The coaches who improve fastest tend to share habits:

  • Get licensed and stay licensed. U.S. Soccer's coaching education ladder (Grassroots → D → C → B → A → Pro) is the formal path. The C and B Licenses are where most full-time youth coaches live; the A is the ceiling for most youth roles.
  • Watch and re-watch professional women's soccer. Not for highlights — for repeated, structured viewing of one position at a time.
  • Have a session-design notebook. Write down what worked, what didn't, why. Rotate sessions deliberately rather than reusing favorites.
  • Have a coaching mentor. Ideally someone two licenses ahead of you, ideally someone who watches you at least twice a year.
  • Read outside soccer. Most of the recent good thinking on player development comes from skill acquisition, motor learning, and education research, not from soccer books.

The standard you can hold yourself to

At the end of every season, ask: are these players better players, healthier humans, and more in love with the game than they were ten months ago? Two out of three is good. Three out of three is great. None is a problem with the coaching, not the players.
